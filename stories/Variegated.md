# Variegated

**By Jason Molina**, with lore by Michael Go.

*A novel of Ranlean, 21 chapters. Aldos Podrigo is Unmorrowed: a boy with no mana in Ranlean, a city where mana is currency. At the Meridian Institute he finds a third language between magic and machinery, alchemy, and two friends, Bren Malric and Meli Salwen. It is the story of how the man the Kori tales know as Dr. Podrigo, the Rot Wizard, began.*

*Lore: [Tales and Campaigns](../ARTHERIA.md#tales-and-campaigns) · see also [Kori: Vampiric Parasite](../ARTHERIA.md#kori-vampiric-parasite), [the Meridian Institute](../ARTHERIA.md#the-meridian-institute)*

> **Note.** In the Kori tales, "Morrowed" and "Unmorrowed" turn into slurs after the Konnor Case. Here the word is already whispered about Aldos as a child.

## Chapter 1

By the time Aldos Podrigo turned twelve, he’d already accepted the world wasn’t built for him. In Ranlean, mana was currency. It bought praise. Respect. Futures. And in that sense, Aldos was poor. Magic was expected here. Children levitated inkpots mid-sentence, stitched enchantments into their uniforms, and bent temperature with a flick of the wrist. Even the clocks ran on spellcraft. The air itself carried a faint charge, like the moment just after lightning—alive, expectant.

Aldos had none of it. He was Unmorrowed. Not cursed, not broken. Just untouched. A blank slate in a city of bright signatures. To be Unmorrowed meant your soul carried no spark, no trace of the divine ember people called mana. Families whispered about it as if it were contagious. Some called them “yesterdays”—people with no place in tomorrow. Others used softer words: practical, ordinary, stable. But everyone agreed: Unmorrowed were meant to live in the margins. They could not heal, conjure, or defend. They tilled fields, pushed carts, and kept ledgers, while their gifted kin carved the future.

Professors didn’t mock him. They simply looked through him, as if he were already absent from the tomorrow they envisioned. He didn’t try to cast anymore. He tried to understand.

At the Meridian Institute, failure was never loud. It was quiet. Practiced. Polished into silence. The most prestigious school in Ranlean even taught disappointment to stand tall. Aldos arrived early most mornings—not for attention, but because the chalk wheel on the casting dais jammed every fourth turn. Back home, you fixed what squeaked before it snapped. His father never waited for machines to complain.

The Podrigo Repair & Salvage Shop sat on a narrow corner of West Ranlean, its windows fogged in winter and crowded year-round with ticking clocks and brass contraptions that hadn’t been in style for fifty years. The air smelled faintly of warmed oil and old metal. A hanging bell over the door chimed differently depending on who opened it—his mother had tuned it that way herself. They worked in the old way: gears, oil, patient hands. No conjured shortcuts. No mana surge to reset what time had worn down. “Magic’s a fast fix,” his father would say, setting a screwdriver between his teeth. “But the quick way isn’t always the right way.” Bruno, Aldos’s older brother, had never quite agreed.

Bruno was born with magic—a quiet marvel in a family that had none. Customers loved when he helped in the shop; he could restore ten orders before lunch with a flick of his wrist. Aldos learned to measure by hand. Bruno learned to skip steps. One day, the shop changed. It was the sort of change no one spoke of directly afterward—just a sudden absence, a shift in the air when you opened the door. The windows were replaced, the bell rehung, the counters polished until they no longer smelled like oil. And Bruno was gone. Aldos didn’t hate him. He just hated the thing that had made it possible. How it had taken his parents, and with them, the brother he’d once known.

At the Institute, Aldos noticed every flaw in the school’s equipment. The screw on the projection arm came loose once a week. The south stairwell railing buzzed with enough static to make younger students flinch. Aldos fixed them. Quietly. Without being asked. Never taking credit. His hands—narrow, ink embedded deep in the creases—moved like he was winding a watch. Thick, dark hair fell forward when he worked, always in the way, always ignored. Chalk dust clung to his sleeves, graphite smudged his fingertips, as if he’d been drafted into a private war against entropy. In his satchel, alongside schoolwork, he carried a second notebook—tucked away like a secret. In it: sketches of spell failures, notes on heat displacement near active glyphs, diagrams of magical feedback reframed in mechanical terms. He didn’t believe in miracles. But he believed in patterns. And somewhere between magic and science, he suspected a third language—one no one else had thought to speak. Alchemy. Not the shimmering illusions of spellcraft, nor the cold calculations of machinery, but a grammar of matter itself. Where mages bent energy to their will, alchemists sought to unweave the threads beneath it, to trade, separate, or combine what already was. It was older than mana, older than Ranlean itself, but in a city that worshipped spellcraft, it had been left to gather dust at the margins.

The old planetarium became his favorite place. Most of its equipment had been abandoned after the city built newer, fully enchanted domes. The school kept this one for tradition. At its center stood a projector from Ranlean’s hybrid era—when mechanical systems were enhanced, not replaced, by magic. Most students didn’t care how it worked. Aldos did. Because it was one of the few machines in the school that spoke both languages. Over several quiet weeks, he cleaned its lenses, oiled its gears, re-scribed the glyphplate in sharper lines, calibrated the rotation so the stars would no longer jitter. No one noticed. That was fine. His mother would’ve hated how dusty the glyphplate had been.

When the day came for the upper-year casting rehearsal, the lecture hall filled with students, teachers, and the soft murmurs of mana being readied. Aldos sat in the back. He wasn’t supposed to be there. But no one saw him slip in. No one ever did. The lights dimmed. The dome came alive. Stars unfolded across the ceiling in slow, perfect arcs. The lenses clicked on cue. The Celestial Array spilled light over the students’ faces like anointing oil. No flickers. No delay. Just motion and glow and breathless silence. Everyone applauded the presenters. No one clapped for the machine. No one knew it had been broken.

After the hall cleared, Aldos stayed. He walked the perimeter once, just to hear the projector cool—the soft tick of heat sinking into brass. He opened his second notebook, flipped past the formulas, and paused on a single hand-drawn sketch: a common levitation glyph. Redrawn in mirror. Modified with notes about resonance, timing, pressure. Not magic. A hypothesis. He tapped the corner of the page with one chalk-dusted fingertip. Then closed the book. One row ahead, a half-elven girl rose from her seat. Quiet. Unfamiliar. Her hair was pale gold, catching the dome’s light as if the stars themselves had brushed against it. For the briefest second, the light around her seemed to ripple—too soft to be deliberate, yet too perfect to be an accident. Her gaze moved from the projector…to him. It didn’t linger. She said nothing. But she had noticed. And Aldos, alone in the dark, allowed himself a small, secret smile. Not because he’d been seen. But because, for once, something he touched had moved the stars.

## Chapter 2

He thought about it for days. The way the stars had shifted. The way the projector had faltered. The way a single touch—a single change—had bent something larger than himself. It wasn’t enough to move the stars once. He needed to understand why. How.

The library smelled faintly of old paper, singed dust, and the metallic tang of mercury from the nearby lamps.  Somewhere deep in the stacks, a clock ticked—too slow, dragging its hands through syrup. Aldos hunched over a scarred wooden desk, his shoulders curled like a shield around the experiment. His soot-smudged spectacles caught a muted glint from the lamp overhead. Thin, pale fingers—crosshatched with old burns and ink stains—steadied a beaker as a thread of viscous silver slid from pipette to dish. The mercury ribbon moved like liquid glass, pooling without a ripple. The others had gone. The professors, the gifted, the heirs of magic bloodlines—off to work that would be remembered. No one noticed the boy who stayed behind. No one saw the rows of failed attempts hidden beneath his sleeves. Magic was the bedrock Ranlean was built upon—shiny, revered, untouchable. But knowledge could be mined from what settled beneath it. And Aldos had begun digging.

It began with Salt. Not the kind scraped from docks, but the white-gold crystals of Aralon—gathered grain by grain from the still flats, each fleck a record of patience and preservation. Salt was soul: unhurried, enduring, molded by its surroundings. He built a lattice from crushed salt crystals, suspending them in threads of Mercury—Ranlean’s own, drawn from the mirrored lakes that powered its lamps and laboratories. Liquid thought: quick, restless, always finding the smallest path forward. And then came Sulfur, mined deep beneath Amethyst Peak. Sulfur burned hot and sour, clinging to everything it touched. It was body—transformative, volatile, impossible to ignore. Alone, nothing. Together—balanced just right—they obeyed. When he applied the right mathematical sequence—not a spell, but a formula—the lattice hovered. Only for a second. But it was enough.

Most days, Aldos worked alone. Sometimes, though, someone passed the doorway—heading toward the upper halls or dismissed early from the observatory decks. And every time, she walked by. Quiet. Still unnamed in his mind. Her pace was never hurried, never idle—measured, almost deliberate. A faint aura glinted at her sleeves and collar as she passed: not lamp light, but magic stitched into fabric. Soft as morning mist, it caught the corner of his vision every time. He didn’t know her name. In his head, she became Miss Shimmer. She never looked inside. Still, he straightened when she passed.

A week later, the door burst open with the confidence of someone who’d never once considered being quiet. Aldos jerked, mercury sloshing near the rim. “Woah,” the boy said, striding in. Broad shoulders under a sleeveless training tunic, leather bracers smudged with chalk and sweat. Dark hair tied back in a rough knot. Bronze skin from days spent outdoors. “A suspended salt array,” he grinned, like he’d stumbled into treasure. “The mercury’s to keep it stable, right? …Why does it stink?”

“Sulfur,” Aldos said flatly. “It tempers the structure.”

“Yeah, well, my nose has a temper now.”

Aldos didn’t look up, but he didn’t tell him to leave.

After a beat: “I’m Bren Malric. Training division. Rune Knight path.”

“Aldos. Podrigo.”

Bren’s grin sharpened. “You’re the one who fixed the projector, right?”

“…That wasn’t on purpose.”

“Doesn’t matter,” Bren said easily. “It worked.”

After that, Bren just… kept showing up. No schedule—he’d wander in after drills, smelling faintly of smoke and training salts, drop into the opposite chair without asking. He understood things. Not just questions—answers too. When Aldos explained that sulfur was causing premature destabilization, Bren crouched to watch the faint halo of heat at the prism’s edge.

“What if it’s collapsing inward?” he said, shifting his stance. Outer foot braced, inner foot guiding the return—like he was meeting an opponent head-on.

“You brace the edges, not the center. Same as taking a hit. Outside takes the shock, inside delivers it back.”

“…Peripheral bracing,” Aldos murmured, scribbling the words.

“Ma taught me that,” Bren said, straightening. “Fight like you’re part of the ground. Trust your senses first, then your training. Pa taught me the rest—respect, keeping your word when it’s the hard choice.”

Aldos kept writing, but the words lodged in his mind.

The lattice lasted nearly fifteen minutes one afternoon—rotating slow, catching sunlight in silver threads. Two copper nodes swung close, almost touching, then parted again. Over and over. He hadn’t built it for beauty, but it felt inevitable. Like it had been waiting to happen. No spell. No bloodline. Just a boy who refused to believe he couldn’t. He thought of Bruno—how shortcuts looked like progress until they broke.

The next morning, frost curled at the library windows. Professors blamed unstable runes. The room was reset. Aldos said nothing. Walking home past the Rune Court, he stopped at the cheers. Bren was in the ring, moving like a dancer disguised as a warrior. He parried, ducked, spun, struck clean. The crowd roared. Bren flushed, scratching the back of his neck like he didn’t know what to do with praise. Aldos smiled faintly. Bren was built for the field. Aldos, for the forge behind it. No one noticed him beneath the mercury lamps. But something in him stayed lit. It wasn’t magic. Not really. But it was his. And it was real.

## Chapter 3

The levitation prism spun as if it had always known how. It had taken twelve failures, one cracked desk, and a brief identity crisis. But it floated now—perfectly. And Aldos… felt lighter too. He no longer hovered near the project nervously or measured its every movement with calipers and held breath. It had settled into the room’s rhythm—like a lamp. Or a friend.  Which meant it was time to move on.

Now his chalk etched circles, not cables. His notes filled with heat ratios and chemical bonds—but also with a new column: influence. What effect a reaction had not just on its parts, but on the pulse inside them. Things like corrosion. Sickness. Rot.

Across the lab, Bren was acting out a parry again—one foot planted, the other pivoting in a half-step, shoulders angled as if fending off an invisible strike.

“See,” he said, shifting his stance mid-demonstration, “this is where you’d feel it. Right here in your ribs, so you push back through your heel and not your spine. Pa always says—”

He stopped, realizing Aldos wasn’t looking.

“I’m listening,” Aldos murmured, tightening the mercury filament around a containment dish.

Inside, a root sample quivered—slightly charred along one edge, but still pulsing with a faint alchemical glow. The mold had shrunk, coiled in on itself.

Aldos glanced briefly at Bren’s stance—not the movements, but the certainty behind them. As if Bren had rehearsed it a hundred times without ever writing it down.

“So what are we hoping for?” Bren asked, stepping closer. “That the root rot will… grow away?”

“I’m hoping,” Aldos said, adjusting the coil, “to take the rot out of the root.”

He marked another symbol beside the copper circle—one resembling a stylized ripple—and scratched quickly into the margin of his journal: What can be drawn out—without breaking what remains?

That morning, in the hallway outside the lab, Aldos had noticed something strange. That half-elven girl was walking with a group of students—five, maybe six—down the main stairwell. Except… two of them had moved in perfect unison. Turned the same way at the same time. Even their hair caught the light the same—like a reflection she’d walked away from. By the time he blinked, they were gone, and she was alone again. It left behind a faint trail of light along the stairwell railing, the way sunlight lingers after a passing cloud. He didn’t know what it meant. Not yet.

A creak from the wooden floorboard at the entryway pulled him back. From across the lab, Bren smirked.

“Oh look, Miss Shimmer’s back.”

“BREN!” Aldos’s voice cracked through the room—too fast, too sharp—his grip on the pen faltering, ink skidding across the page in a black slash.

At the doorway, the girl stopped short—not just paused, but froze, mid-step. She glanced behind her, then pointed to herself, brows furrowed.

“Me? My name’s Meli.”

Bren grinned. “That’s just what he calls yo—”

Aldos shot him a look sharp enough to end the sentence.

She stepped inside, gaze moving past the chalkboard and the root dish until it landed on the prism.

“Oh…” she breathed. “Is that floating?”

Aldos glanced up. “Yes.”

She stepped closer. “It’s not like a levitation spell. It’s… steadier. Quieter.”

“It’s built to hold balance,” he said. “Not defy it.”

Meli tilted her head, watching it spin.

“It looks like it’s listening to something,” she said. “Like it knows where it wants to be.”

She lifted a hand toward it, but paused. Aldos hesitated. Then gave a small nod.

Her fingers stopped just shy of the prism. The air between them rippled faintly—not from heat, but from the soft refraction of light, like rainwater catching the sun. A small bloom of magic unfurled—petals of pale, translucent color, like orchids sculpted from glass. But instead of drifting aimlessly, the petals aligned themselves to the prism’s path, orbiting in time with its spin. Each turn of the array drew them along, and each flicker of her magic adjusted to match its rhythm. For the first time, alchemy and magic didn’t stand apart. They moved with each other.

Neither leading nor following—balanced in the same space. He’d built it to stand alone. Now, for the first time, he let someone else touch its balance. He didn’t breathe. He didn’t want to break it. When the petals finally faded, the prism kept its steady spin, but the air felt changed—as if the room itself remembered them working together.

“That was just for fun,” she said softly. “I like when things feel included.”

Aldos blinked once, twice. “…Thanks.”

From the far side of the lab, Bren reappeared with two mismatched mugs.

“The tea’s bitter. Perfect for you. You’re welcome.”

Aldos barely registered the jab.

Meli giggled at their sparring, the sound quick and bright. She tucked a strand of hair behind her ear and said, almost as an afterthought,

“Clear skies today… means the stars will have something to say tonight.”

He was still watching the space where the light had danced. That night, long after the others had gone, he sat alone again in the same chair. The prism still hovered. So did her words. He didn’t know what they meant. Only that they would stay.

## Chapter 4

Aldos always waited until the classroom emptied, until the last incantation faded from the air and the world returned to reason. The room still held its aftertaste—chalk runes smudged but not gone, heat lines trembling where an illusion had recently bloomed. On the desk beside him, yesterday’s levitation array hovered with stubborn precision. Today, he was after something more difficult. A violet tincture inched down a pipette toward a copper coil. One drop at a time. The flame beneath flickered—low, deliberate. His journal lay open, its margins crowded with ink-blotted equations and scorched notes, the paper curling faintly at the edges. He didn’t blink when the door opened. He was nearly at the final drop when—

“You’re not going to the observatory?”

He didn’t look up.

“No.”

Not rude. Just focused. His knuckles flexed on the pipette, as if steadying both the glass and himself. Aldos knew the voice. Of course he did. He’d memorized its cadences before he ever intended to.

Meli stood in the doorway, robes slightly long in the sleeves, a shimmer still fading from her palms—residue from her usual magic, quiet and light. She never tried to stand out, and somehow that made her impossible to ignore.

“It’s the Array,” she said.

“I know.”

“They’ve improved the resonance pattern. Shouldn’t crack this year.”

“I’ve seen the adjustments,” he replied. “But magic always finds new ways to break. Especially when people are watching.”

She didn’t laugh, but she didn’t leave either.

“Why do you always stay behind?”

He kept his eye on the tincture.

“Because this is the only place where what you get depends on what you put in.”

She tilted her head slightly.

He gestured to the coil.

“This will glow green because I made it that way. If it doesn’t, I’ll know why. No surge. No misfire. No talent required. Just reaction and result.”

“That’s alchemy, right?”

He finally looked at her.

“Yeah. Equal exchange.”

“You give something. You get something.”

“Exactly.” He wiped the rim of the tube with a cloth. “It’s fair.”

She stepped inside, moving carefully, and sat beside him—far enough not to interfere, close enough to be present.

“I could show you the Array,” she said. “From here.”

He raised an eyebrow.

“You can replicate it?”

“Sort of. Through a minor illusion. Won’t be perfect, but it’ll hold.”

She shaped the circle slowly—thumb to ring finger, index trailing the air. A shimmer bloomed like heat off stone. Gradually, the Array appeared: glowing spheres in orbit, glyphs spinning on invisible axes, threads of starlight tethering motion to geometry. Two comets arced across the projection’s sky, tails streaming long and luminous until, for a moment, their paths overlapped. The light swelled at the crossing—warmer now, humming faintly, as if the air itself remembered something.

Meli smiled faintly.

“Like the stars are aligning.”

Aldos’ breath caught. The shape was familiar—like an equation he’d solved before. No one had ever shown him magic like this. Not to impress. Not to intimidate. Just… to share. Her magic didn’t demand attention. It made space for two. One of the spheres jittered. He flinched. She corrected it without comment.

“It’s always beautiful,” he said, mostly to himself. “Until it breaks.”

She glanced at him.

“A beautiful disaster,” he added. “Waiting for applause.”

She let the illusion settle into a quiet, stable loop.

“I don’t hate magic,” he said. “I hate that it picks people. That it hands them something before they’ve earned it. And then makes the rest of us feel like we were never supposed to understand.”

She didn’t argue. She just kept the projection alive.

After a moment, she asked,

“Do you hate us, Aldos?”

He looked at her. Really looked.

“…No,” he said.

A beat.

“Well—not you.”

## Chapter 5

The city spoke in anticipation. Lanterns pulsed in time with countdown glyphs. Children wore mirrored masks shaped like comets. Someone in the crowd hummed a tune—half lullaby, half hymn—that threaded through the chatter. Aldos knew the story. Everyone did. It wasn’t taught so much as absorbed—whispered between lecture halls and skywatch platforms, baked into star-shaped pastries sold only once a year.

Vellica burned gentle, a calm silver tail that glided through the stars like a falling thought. Named for Vellichor, the god of memory and meaning—keeper of lost words, guardian of fading things. He was the one who watched quietly while the world changed, and wrote it down anyway. Thorne was chaos. A flare that licked across the sky without pattern—too bright, too close, too loud. Born of Anarch’s breath, the god who carved mountains with his fists and dared the dragons to wake. He was motion without map, the scream at the edge of every beginning. And then, once—only once—they crossed.

Over Ranlean, thousands of years ago, for less than a minute, Vellica’s calm and Thorne’s fire aligned. Their opposing lights—storm and stillness—folded into each other, equalized. And for a moment, the sky held something neither of them could create alone. Something the old myths never named.

The Celestial Array.

It had never happened again. But every year, the city remembered.

Behind the scaffolding, Aldos crouched low, one hand on the resistance coil, the other gripping the framework to steady himself. The rig thrummed faintly under his touch—metal warm, magic vibrating through the brass filigree. Every year, the Celestial Array ended the same way: too fast, too loud, too close. The projections of Vellica and Thorne—mana-fed illusions—rushed toward one another, their light flaring in a burst of conjured starlight before fading into applause. The crowd always cheered. But Aldos had run the numbers. Calculated the arc lengths. Mapped the approach angles. They were just illusions, sure. Architected spelllight. But the systems behind them were real. Coils. Refractors. Threaded conduits pulsing behind stone and glass older than most of the staff running it. The spellforms didn’t align—they converged. At those speeds, even a fraction too close could trigger feedback: thermal flare, projection crash, system rupture. He’d flagged it once. No one listened. They’d been doing it for hundreds of years, after all. This year, he’d made adjustments. Rewrote timing glyphs. Installed a dampener behind the alignment rig. When the comets drew close, he’d slow them. Not much. Just enough. Let the illusion stretch. Let it breathe. Make it safe. And maybe…make it beautiful.

The comets began their final approach. From the crowd, cheers rose like wind over stone. Children pointed, silver masks flashing in the light. Somewhere, a conductor raised a baton and the music surged—louder, faster, swelling toward the climax everyone expected. But then… the sky hesitated. Just before the crossing, the light shifted. The projections slowed—not a malfunction, not quite—but enough to make people hold their breath. Enough to feel it. Vellica glided in like a breath drawn in prayer. Thorne flared, then softened, its ragged blaze evening out into steady gold. They circled, eclipsed, became one. The illusion stretched—not a flash, but a moment. One long, impossible second of perfect light…and in that pause, the crowd forgot to cheer. In that moment, Meli turned. She wasn’t looking at the sky. She was looking backstage. Through the framework and sigil glass, she caught a glimpse of him—shadow bent over the coil, the faint glint of brass in his hands. Just enough to know.

The air still held the faint aftertaste of their shared experiment in the lab. She smiled—not the kind meant to be seen, but the kind that slips out when you realize you were right.

“Of course it was you,” she whispered.

Aldos didn’t hear the words, but he saw the way her mouth shaped them. And though the crowd roared when the moment broke, he kept his eyes on hers until the light faded—letting her have the last second of the Array, just for them.

## Chapter 6

Four years later, the Celestial Array was no longer something they watched—it was something they built. In the years between, mornings had turned into rehearsals, and rehearsals into precision workdays. Their roles had settled naturally—Meli guiding the light, Aldos guarding the seams. Meli stood beneath the dome as one of the show’s support illusionists, her hands guiding radiant threads of spelllight into constellations so precise they felt inevitable. She moved with quiet confidence, the sky bending to her will. Backstage, out of view, Aldos tightened a copper band around a mana coil. A screen above him flickered. A small tremor in the western stabilizer. He adjusted the cooling glyph by a quarter-inch and watched the readings fall back to green. No alarms. No backlash. She carved beauty from magic. He kept it from burning the world down.

In the quieter hours of morning, Aldos returned to his lab. The school had granted him a space of his own last year—an entire west-wing greenhouse that had once been used for herbal studies, now retrofitted with distilled mercury conduits, projection tables, and arrays of micro-circle filtration tanks. Most visitors saw the plants first. Rows of vibrant greens, delicate purples, and flowering ferns draped across every shelf and beam. Some joked it looked more like a nursery than a lab. But Aldos knew better. Beneath every pot was a transmutation circle. Etched into ceramic. Chalked onto copper. Painted into the wood. Every plant had once been rotting, sickened by fungus or frost or the steady march of time. Aldos didn’t cure them. He removed what didn’t belong.

That morning, he hovered over a particularly stubborn fern, its veins blackened at the tips. A mercury solution swirled beneath it, tethered to a siphoning array. He moved slowly, drawing out a tangle of disease with a pair of etched tongs. Behind him, the door opened with a soft hiss.

“You’ve gotten good at that,” said Bren.

Aldos didn’t look away from the fern.

“Thanks. But the technique could be better.”

Bren stepped in, shedding his training jacket, now soaked in sweat.

“Well, it’s good enough for the hospitals. They’re already using it to remove minor illnesses.”

Aldos extracted the last strand of black and watched it dissolve in the basin.

“Yeah,” he said. “That’s pretty cool, huh?”

He glanced over his shoulder.

“You look beat.”

“Training’s been rough. The instructors are brutal.” Bren laughed weakly. “I think they’d declare sleep a national weakness if they could.”

Aldos smiled, half-tilted.

“They’re not wrong.”

Bren leaned against the table, quiet for a while. Then, almost offhand:

“When you perfect your technique… do you think you could take the fear out of a knight?”

Aldos let out a quiet chuckle.

Then paused.

He turned back to the fern. No rot left. No errors. Just a clean, green thing in a pot.

“…Maybe,” he said. “I’d have to isolate it first.”

Bren exhaled softly, then shrugged.

“I’m just kidding,” he said, though it didn’t sound much like a joke. “Not sure taking fear out would even help.”

Aldos didn’t answer. He just stared at the basin, the last of the disease dissolving beneath the surface.

Later that week, while calibrating a rotational array beneath a skyvine, someone knocked lightly on the lab’s frame. A student stepped in, carrying a small pot with bright green leaves.

“Aldos?”

He kept his focus on the array.

“Yes?”

“I brought you something. It’s a variegated monstera. The roots were a little damaged, but I cleaned them up. I thought you’d like it.”

They stepped forward shyly.

“You love plants.”

Aldos accepted the pot, still bent over the runes. Without lifting his head, without glancing behind him:

“No, I don’t.”

The student froze. Behind him, sunlight filtered through a jungle—rows upon rows of vibrant growth, climbing shelves and soaking in the light like worshipers at a quiet shrine.

“…Right,” the student mumbled. “Well. It’s yours now.”

Aldos nodded.

“Thank you.”

He returned to his work. He never once looked up to see the irony of it all.

That evening, he sat alone in the greenhouse, palms pressed to either side of a tiny sapling. There was nothing wrong with it. No rot. No mold. No parasites. Just green, unbroken life. Across the room, the variegated monstera leaned toward the lamplight, its leaves unfurling in slow, deliberate silence. The white streaks weren’t uniform—too much, too little, never quite balanced. A flaw, technically. But maybe beauty didn’t need balance. Maybe it was the uneven streaks that made the leaf worth looking at. And for the first time, he wondered—if rot could be lifted away, could light be lifted too? Could the thing that made something worth keeping be made to bloom in someone else? He didn’t have an answer. But he kept looking at the monstera long after the sun went down.

## Chapter 7

Aldos sat in his lab. By this point, the levitation array had already forgotten gravity existed. It floated in perfect, effortless rhythm above his desk—untethered, untroubled. He didn’t use it for anything anymore. It no longer served a function. But it stayed. A symbol of accomplishment. A lucky charm. Proof that some things, once balanced just right, could hold their shape without intervention. And sometimes, when the lab was quiet, he caught himself just…watching it.

He was sketching a vessel diagram when the knock came—soft, even, not urgent. Definitely not Bren. He opened the door. Meli stood there with a canteen slung over one shoulder and a folded map in her hand. She was wind-flushed, cheeks warm from the cold air, and grinning.

“You’re coming with me,” she said.

Meli’s shoulders hitched, then lowered half an inch.

A proctor slipped past them, hand hovering near her elbow.

“Miss Selwan,” she murmured. “We’re going to dim the hall. Take your time.”

The lamps softened.

The not-people thinned like fog at noon.

When the hush finally broke, it was with real voices: the marshal ushering people out, the sponsors speaking in calm, careful tones, Bren exhaling for what sounded like the first time in a minute.

Aldos didn’t move until Meli did. He stayed at the edge of the circle, hands at his sides, eyes steady on hers as if that were a rope she could hold.

She opened her eyes like someone surfacing.

He didn’t ask, Are you okay? He asked, softly,

“Do you want to breathe with me?”

She nodded.

So they did.

And around them, the hall remembered what was real.

## Chapter 11

Meli opened her eyes.

The last thing she saw before the dark took her was a wall of faces closing in. Now—Aldos’s face filled her vision instead. Steady, kind, and impossibly close. For a moment, she thought she was still dreaming. No one ever looked at her like that. His expression wasn’t pity or worry. It was warmth. Relief. Love, even.

Her chest ached from the memory of the panic, but the air came easier now. She exhaled and let him pull her into a hug—the kind of embrace that returned the stolen breath. When she finally released him, she noticed Bren standing just behind, hands folded on his head, in awe.

“That was powerful,” Bren said, shaking his head in disbelief.

Meli blinked at him.

“Nothing about that was powerful, Bren. I’m weak.”

“No, honey,” one of the proctors interjected, kneeling down beside her. “He’s right. As frightening as it must have been for you, it was just as impressive. You didn’t just conjure illusions—you overlaid them onto the hall itself. You gave them weight.”

Bren leaned forward, nodding.

“Yeah. I really felt like I was pushing through people. But when I looked back… it was just the stands…might have to get those fixed.”

Meli swallowed hard, her face flushing. She remembered flashes—the music rising, the pressure in her chest, then shadows filling the spaces between the seats. At first it was just a few figures in the periphery, so natural she thought they’d always been there. But then they grew. They pressed closer. Whispers rising, hands too near, faces too many. Until she couldn’t see Aldos or Bren at all. Until her heart drowned out everything else.

She shivered. “I didn’t mean to.”

“Of course you didn’t,” the proctor said, placing a hand gently on her shoulder. “But intent doesn’t change what we witnessed. The skill required for what you just did—layering illusion over structure seamlessly—is rare. Very rare.” She paused, considering. “After you’ve had time to rest, I’ll be reaching out about some opportunities for you.”

Meli stared at the ground, shame and disbelief tangling in her stomach. Opportunities. From what felt like her failure.

Aldos touched her arm, grounding her again. She dared a glance at him, and the steadiness in his eyes was unbearable in the best way. It said you’re safe. You’re enough. She held onto it like a lifeline.

“Funny thing is,” Bren muttered, still half-grinning, “I didn’t even realize it was fake until we lost you.”

The words twisted in Meli’s chest.

Lost.

Yes. That was exactly what it felt like.

## Chapter 12

Corridors echoing, labs cooling, chalk dust settling like snow that nobody would disturb until morning. The Institute always felt thinner on holidays. Meli had left early with a basket and a promise to spend the night at her mother’s; she’d squeezed Aldos’s hand at the gate and said,

“Tomorrow,”

Like a vow.

An hour later Bren appeared in Aldos’s doorway with a scarf around his neck and a grin that could have started a parade.

“Hearthfall at my parents’,” he said. “You’re coming.”

“I was going to recalibrate the—”

Bren was already lifting Aldos’s coat from the peg.

“You’re not giving me a choice, are you?”

“Not even a little.”

They cut through streets strung with lantern wreaths—circles of mercury glass and paper holly that pulsed slowly in time with breath and song. Vendors sang, kettles steamed, children chased illusions of paper foxes that darted through ankles and dissolved when caught. Bren flowed through it like he’d been born to; Aldos tucked in behind, dodging a boy with a tray of candied pears, then failing to dodge the boy’s second tray of candied pears until Bren’s hand caught his sleeve.

“Head up, Podrigo,” Bren said, laughing. “Mom’s making wreathfire dumplings!”

The Malric home sat on a stepped hill of rowhouses—stone warmed to silver by the streetlamps, ivy braided with ribbon. Bren didn’t knock so much as announce himself through the door.

“Ma! Pa! I brought a stray!”

The reply was a hurricane and a hymn.

A woman barrelled out of the kitchen with an oven cloth over one shoulder and flour on her cheek. She was broad-shouldered like her son, slate-gray skin marbled in darker swirls, hair twisted up with a twig of holly jammed into it like a flag. Her smile could have lit the street.

“Bren Malric, if you track mud on my clean floor I will use you as the door mat,” she said, and then she had Aldos in a hug that rearranged his ribs. “And you must be Aldos. Look at those polite eyes. Come in. Shoes off. Hands washed. Dagan! He’s tiny!”

A tall, quiet man appeared behind her, wiping his hands on a towel—stone-still presence, warm eyes.

“I’m Dagan,” he said, offering a palm that was big and careful. “Welcome.”

“Lysa,” the woman added, ruffling Aldos’s hair like she’d known him ten years. “Sit. Eat. Talk. Not in that order.”

The house smelled like cardamom and roasted root vegetables. Garlands looped between beams; rune candles hummed low; on one wall, a row of clay tiles bore handprints in different sizes, pressed over the years into a river of palms.

Bren’s mother swung back into the kitchen, issuing orders like a general.

“Bren, chairs. Dagan, please don’t let the dumplings escape again. Aldos—sweetheart, are you allergic to anything? Does your face swell if I feed you shrimp? Blink twice if yes.”

“I—don’t think so,” Aldos said, blinking once.

“Good. Set spoons.”

He moved automatically to help. The middle cabinet door squeaked as he reached for bowls; without thinking he knelt, tugged a tiny screwdriver from his pocket, and adjusted the hinge pin a quarter-turn. The squeak vanished.

Lysa stopped in the doorway and pointed with a ladle.

“See that? He hears the squeak before it starts. Why don’t you think more like Aldos, Bren?”

“Ma,” Bren groaned, hauling two chairs under one arm. “He lives in a lab. He hears atoms squeak.”

Dagan’s mouth softened into a smile.

“Polite, observant, good listener,” he said. “You picked a smart friend, son.”

Heat crept up Aldos’s neck.

“He’s the reason I made it here at all,” he said, helplessly truthful.

“And I drag him out of his cave,” Bren added, chest out. “Team effort.”

Dinner was loud in the best way. Stew and braided bread and dumplings that absolutely tried to escape. Lysa peppered Aldos with questions between refills—

“Do you like the Institute? Do you sleep? Do they make you eat enough? Eat more.”—and Dagan asked the kind of quiet questions that made answers feel like they mattered.

At one point Lysa thumped the table lightly with the back of her spoon.

“See that? He puts his fork down before talking. Why don’t you—”

“Maaa!”

“—ever put your fork down, Bren?”

Bren looked offended on principle and kept eating. Everyone laughed, the kind that resets a room. Aldos felt something unclench he hadn’t realized was clenched—a shape inside him loosening into the space they made around this table.

Then Bren’s cousins arrived—two, then a third, then an aunt with a pie and opinions. The kitchen compressed, voices layered, dishes passed the wrong way, a toddler attempted a jail break under the table. Aldos’s pulse ticked up; the overlapping sound had the same too-many-variables quality as a misaligned rig.

Bren saw it like he always did. One big hand landed steady on Aldos’s shoulder, not pinning, just there.

“Breathe,” he said, so low it didn’t have to be heard to work. “You’re good.”

He peeled off to intercept the aunt who had a question shaped like a pry bar about Aldos’s parents.

“Ma,” Bren said, louder. “Tell Aldos the story of the year your dumplings set the curtains on fire.”

“They didn’t,” Lysa said, scandalized and delighted. “The curtains set the dumplings on fire. Entirely different.”

Aldos grinned, tension dissolving like sugar in tea.

After dishes (Aldos insisted on washing; Lysa relented only when Dagan declared that “letting people help is also a skill”), they moved to the front room for gifts. Bren presented his parents with a wrapped bundle; Lysa unrolled it to reveal two rune-threaded mitts stitched with the Malric family sign.

“So you stop burning your hands,” he mumbled, suddenly shy.

She made a noise somewhere between a laugh and a sob and kissed his forehead, flour and all.

Aldos cleared his throat.

“It’s nothing big,” he said, offering a small parcel.

Inside lay a brass-and-glass music box, repaired to gleam, with a tiny etches on the lid. When Lysa turned the key, stars spun softly inside the dome—two tiny comets circling and crossing, their light pooling on the ceiling. The tune was simple and old.

Lysa’s hand flew to her mouth. Dagan’s eyes shone.

“Where did you—”

“Found it in a salvage bin,” Aldos said, ears pink. “It was missing teeth. I—fixed it.”

Lysa pressed both palms to his cheeks and kissed him again. “Keep him,” she told Bren. “If he tries to leave, you handcuff him to the radiator.”

The wall of handprint tiles caught Aldos’s eye again. He stepped closer. Names were carved beneath—BREN (messy), Lysa, Dagan, a cluster of cousins, a grandparent.

“We add one each year,” Dagan said, coming to stand beside him. “Family and who feels like it.”

Aldos swallowed. “I don’t—”

“Feel like it,” Dagan finished, gentle. “We know. No rush.” He paused. “But if you wanted to… we’d make space.”

Bren bumped his shoulder, easy as breathing. “He’s with me,” he said. It sounded like a promise and a warning to the world.

They pressed his hand anyway—Aldos’s slim fingers next to Bren’s broad palm—into soft clay, Lysa inscribing ALDOS with a flourish that made it look like it had always been there. Aldos looked away quickly and pretended it was because the lantern needed trimming.

Later, after dessert and a brief family debate about whether dumplings could be legally categorized as soup (Lysa: yes; Dagan: no; Bren: eat them and stop asking questions), the three of them stepped out onto the stoop to breathe the cold.

The neighborhood sang—quiet carols, laughing, the metallic ring of someone stringing last-minute wreaths. Across the street, a community projection draped gentle auroras over real lanterns, hiding a cracked arch so neatly you’d never notice unless you were looking for seams.

Bren followed Aldos’s gaze.

“New mesh,” he said. “They’ve got it layered right over the stone.”

Aldos squinted, admiring the clean integration.

“It anchors to the frame,” he murmured, mind automatically filing away the trick.

“Useful,” Bren added. “Looks better than the real thing.”

“Sometimes,” Aldos said. “Until it hides the part that needs fixing.”

Bren huffed a laugh. “You and your fixes.”

“Someone has to keep the curtains from lighting dumplings,” Aldos said.

Inside, Lysa bellowed, “Boys! Who left the kettle thinking about boiling and not actually boiling?”

Eyes wide, “Are you scared of your mom?”

“No! I’m not scared of anything Podrigo.”

Bren leaned his head back against the door and closed his eyes like a man beginning a prayer.

“Coming!”

He clapped Aldos on the shoulder, then pulled him into a quick, fierce hug that knocked the air out of him in the exact right way.

“You belong here,” Bren said into his hair. “Just so you know.”

Aldos didn’t trust his voice, so he knocked his forehead lightly against Bren’s collarbone, which Bren chose to interpret as agreement.

“Happy Hearthfall,” Bren said with a smile.

They went back in.

Lysa immediately shoved two mugs of spiced tea at them; Dagan passed Aldos a slice of pie like a medal. The music box turned soft and steady on the shelf, comets crossing, and for one warm, ordinary night—under paper holly and rune candles and the loud grace of a family that made room—Aldos let himself unlearn the shape of being alone.

## Chapter 13

The commons had returned to its usual hum after Hearthfall. The garlands were half-taken down, stray bits of wax clung to the tables, and the air still smelled faintly of spice and fried dough. Students drifted back to study nooks and lecture prep, their laughter quieter now, folded back into routine. In their corner table, the three of them sat together, still carrying the aftertaste of the holiday.

Meli spoke first.

“This year was different,” she said, almost like she wasn’t sure she wanted to say it out loud. “When I got home, my mom looked at me—really looked at me. No hesitation, no questions about whether I was the illusion.”

Her eyes softened, a glimmer of joy edged with ache.

“She told me, ‘Illusions don’t give off a smell. And, my love, I will always recognize your scent’”

Meli smiled, quick and fragile, then pressed her palms together on the table.

“It was the first Hearthfall in years where she was fully there. Just her. Just my mom.”

Neither Aldos nor Bren answered right away. They didn’t need to.

Finally, Bren leaned back, propping his boots against the table leg.

“Sounds a lot better than my mother hurling dumplings at me because I eat too fast.”

He grinned at Meli, then flicked his eyes to Aldos.

“Having him there helped. My parents love him. Couldn’t stop saying, ‘See, Bren? Why don’t you think more like Aldos?’”

Meli smirked.

“They’re not wrong.”

Aldos gave a faint, embarrassed shrug, his eyes fixed on his tea cup. He turned it slowly in his hands, as if watching the liquid swirl might keep him from having to meet their gaze.

“It was nice,” he admitted at last, his voice low. “I forgot what it was like. Family, I mean. The noise. The warmth. Even the teasing.”

His shoulders moved almost imperceptibly, like carrying something he wasn’t used to sharing.

Meli tilted her head, studying him.

“We’ve never …” Her tone was careful. “What happened to your family, Aldos?”

Bren leaned forward, softer than usual.

“Yeah. You never mention them.”

For a moment Aldos didn’t answer. He set down his cup, folded his hands together, and pressed them against his notebook like anchoring himself to the table.

“They ran a repair shop,” he said finally. “Not glamorous. Old circuits, broken lamps, failing projectors. My father used to say every broken thing has a voice. If you listen long enough, it tells you where it hurts. My mother believed patience was the real craft. Take the long way. Never force what isn’t ready. We worked without magic. Always.”

He stopped, his breath clipped short.

“But my brother, Bruno… he had magic. Real magic. And he wanted to help. My parents tried to teach him their way—hands first, time first—but for him, it was like asking a bird not to fly. He understood, but…” Aldos’s jaw tightened. “He couldn’t resist the shortcuts. He thought magic could do in seconds what our hands did in hours.”

Meli’s brow furrowed, but she stayed quiet.

“He used to hum when he worked,” Aldos added, voice barely above a whisper. “Little off-key songs. The bench never felt empty when he was there. I still hear it sometimes.”

His throat worked.

“One day, the orders piled high. The bench was full. Customers waiting. Bruno thought he’d help. He wove mana into one of the circuit frames.” Aldos’s eyes flicked down, the memory burning behind them. “It looked clean. Too clean. But mana doesn’t obey like copper. It seeped past the lines, pooled where no one accounted for it.”

He swallowed hard.

“And my parents didn’t account for it. When the overload hit, the shop went silent. Smoke. Then nothing.”

The words sat heavy between them.

“Bruno lived,” Aldos went on, quieter now. “Barely. But the shame ate him alive. He begged me to leave Ranlean with him. Said no one here would forgive him. Said he couldn’t forgive himself.”

Aldos’s voice thinned, but he didn’t falter.

“But I couldn’t go. Someone had to stay. Someone had to make sure it never happened again.”

The table was still. Only the soft hum of mercury lights filled the space.

Aldos’s fingers brushed the edge of his notebook, tracing it like a scar.

“So I stayed. And I learned to take things apart until I knew exactly what didn’t belong. To pull it out clean. To fix what should’ve been safe all along.”

He looked up, briefly—just long enough for Meli to see the grief tucked behind the precision.

Meli slid her hand across the table until it rested on his. Warm. Steady.

Bren didn’t joke this time. He just gave a small nod, like he finally understood something about his friend that had always been hidden under the calculations.

Aldos exhaled, slow.

“It wasn’t just him. None of us saw what could happen.”

For the first time, he’d said it out loud. For the first time, they knew why he carried silence like armor.

And for the first time, he didn’t feel like he was carrying it alone.

## Chapter 14

The campus had slipped back into its rhythm. Garlands from Hearthfall still sagged across the commons like lazy banners, their wax-stained ribbons fluttering in the cold breeze. Students hurried between halls with arms full of books and ink-stained hands, laughter rising in small bursts before being swallowed by the winter air.

Aldos, Meli, and Bren wove through it together. Aldos carried three volumes balanced against his chest — one on rune theory, one on alchemical ratios, and one that was clearly from the astronomy wing. Bren eyed the stack with a grin.

“You know, if you carried any more books, we’d have to apply for a cart permit,” he said.

“I don’t need a cart,” Aldos muttered, adjusting the stack. “These are manageable.”

“Manageable until you sneeze.”

Meli laughed, tugging gently at Aldos’s sleeve.

“He’s right. You’re going to drop them all.”

“I won’t,” Aldos replied, though his ears had already gone pink.

They reached the dining hall and claimed their corner table by the frosted window. The air inside smelled of oil and dough — fried dumplings and hearth-bread still in steady demand even after the holiday. Meli pressed her palms together, almost as if holding in words until they found the right shape.

“After the audition… one of the Array directors spoke to me.”

Both heads turned toward her.

“It wasn’t a promise,” she said quickly, “but they hinted I might have a chance at an apprenticeship. A real position. With stipends, regular practice, a spot in the team’s rotation.” She drew a breath. “If I don’t mess it up.”

“You won’t,” Bren said instantly. No hesitation. Just surety.

Meli tilted her head, half-smiling, half-unconvinced.

“You didn’t see the way my hands shook. What if—”

“They don’t care about that,” Aldos interrupted, his voice firmer than usual. She looked at him, surprised. He set down his tea, met her gaze with quiet precision.

“Your illusions don’t falter. They reveal. That’s rarer than perfect control.”

Her cheeks warmed. She lowered her eyes, but her smile lingered.

Before the silence could deepen, a courier’s boots clattered across the hall. He stopped at their table and produced a sealed envelope stamped with the crest of Amethyst Peak.

“Delivery for Bren Malric.”

Bren broke the seal. His eyes moved quickly over the words, then stopped. He read them again, slower this time. When he finally looked up, the grin wasn’t flashy — it was full, steady, and a little breathless.

“I’m in,” he said.

Meli gasped, clapping her hands together.

“Bren, that’s amazing!”

She leaned over and hugged him before he could react, nearly knocking the letter from his hand.

He laughed, squeezing her back.

“I worked for this. Every drill, every bruise—it was worth it.”

His tone wasn’t arrogant, but grounded. Honest.

“And I couldn’t have done it without you two. You kept me steady. You pushed me.”

“You pushed yourself,”

Meli said softly, pulling back to meet his eyes.

“We just reminded you what you’re capable of.”

Bren’s throat worked, but he only smiled.

Aldos watched them, the corners of his mouth twitching upward. For once, Bren wasn’t joking. And Meli wasn’t doubting. It was rare, and it was real.

Later that night, the three of them sat again at their usual corner table, this time with plates of fried dumplings and mugs of spiced cider.

Bren bit into one and winced.

“Too hot.”

“Then wait,” Aldos said flatly.

“I did wait…barely.”

Aldos stared.

“It felt like years!”

Meli laughed so hard she nearly spilled her drink.

“You’ll never change.”

Bren shot her a grin.

“No way! Only way that’d happen is if someone reached in and rewired me.”

He chuckled, light as ever.

Meli laughed with him, but Aldos’s chest tightened. The words rang too close. Rewired. He looked down quickly, pretending to study the page in front of him.

They ate and joked until the dumplings were gone and the cider mugs empty, until their laughter had gone ragged around the edges. And when the noise finally softened, Meli leaned back, eyes tracing the frost on the window.

Aldos glanced at her. Just a glance. But she caught it — the way his gaze lingered a second too long, the way he looked away quickly when she turned. She smiled to herself, faint and private. Outside, the mercury lamps hummed, their light pooling against the glass. Inside, the three of them sat in the kind of warmth that made the long winter feel shorter.

## Chapter 15

They made the announcement at midday, when the dome light was soft and even and the mercury lamps hummed like a choir holding one note. At the Meridian Institute, assemblies like this meant the whole school stopped—classes dismissed, halls emptied, and students filed into Lecture Hall A.

The Meridian Board stood at the front: three faculty in formal robes, two city sponsors with neat pens, and the same representative whose hair always looked sharp enough to sign documents by itself. A swath of students filled the seats, the murmur of their curiosity settling as the headmaster raised a hand.

“For this centennial Anniversary Array,” he said, voice carrying, “the Institute will field a full student-led illusion team, supervised by faculty and city engineers.”

A brief pause.

“Lead Illusionist: Meli Salwen.”

For a heartbeat the hall didn’t breathe. Then it did. Cheers blooming, boots scuffing stone, palms striking palms. Bren whooped once, clean and loud. Aldos didn’t clap right away. He watched the first flicker pass through Meli’s eyes—disbelief, then the calmer weight that follows joy when it realizes it has work to do. Only then did he let himself smile.

They called her down for the formalities: a folio, a signed seal, a schedule. The Committee rep spoke in that bright careful tone sponsors use when they want to be both inspiring and precise.

“It’s a historic year,” she said. “We’ll integrate your illusions with the hybrid rig—mechanical refractors, alchemical conduits, and spelllight. We chose you because your work holds attention without forcing it.”

A tilt of the head.

“One directive as you build: beauty must never bury structure.”

Meli bowed her head once.

“Understood.”

Aldos, from the aisle, felt the sentence pin itself to his ribs like a rule he already kept. Afterward the hall loosened. Classmates came in waves—congratulations, shoulder squeezes, a few jokes about needing front-row seats. Bren cut through the L-shaped crowd with practiced ease and lifted Meli in a brief, spinning hug that made her laugh for real.

“You earned it,” he said, setting her down. “Youngest lead they’ve named since—ever?”

“Don’t say ever,” she said, grinning despite herself. “Ten years will do.”

He slid a folded envelope from his jacket as if he’d only just remembered it. The Amethyst Peak seal flashed gold in the lamplight.

“They said yes,” Bren added, softer now. “Conditionals met. Spring intake.”

Meli’s cheer was immediate and uncontained. Aldos’s smile was smaller—but it reached the edges of his eyes in a way that made Bren clap him on the back like the news belonged to all three of them.

By the time they escaped the hall, the sun had tipped west. They didn’t go to the ledge or the lake. They went to the lab—Aldos’s greenhouse—because that was where they always went when their lives changed shape. He put on water for tea without asking. Steam climbed the glass. The levitation prism spun above his desk, as constant as a heartbeat. The monstera had unfurled a new leaf overnight, cream streaked through green like frost catching ivy.

Meli set the folio down and traced its edge.

“They want full overlay capability,” she said, more to the air than to either of them. “Illusions that can harmonize with moving parts—no jitter, no drift. I’ll need anchor threads that flex with load changes.”

“You’ll have them,” Aldos said. “I’ll map the rig frequencies so your anchors can ride the natural resonance instead of fighting it.”

He pulled a scrap of chalk toward him.

“If the refractors hum at eighty-seven hertz under peak, I can tune the copper coils to throw a sympathetic counter by five. Think of it like—”

“Like bracing at the edges,” Bren finished, leaning against the bench with a crooked half-smile. “Not the center.”

Aldos huffed a breath that might have been a laugh.

“Exactly.”

Meli’s gaze moved from the prism to the rows of careful work all around them: siphons, coils, hand-sketched arrays.

“I can also soften seams when we need the sky to feel continuous,” she said, thinking aloud. “Hide joints. Smooth the feeling of the dome’s edge so the audience lives inside the piece instead of watching it.”

Aldos’s pencil paused for a heartbeat, then continued.

“So long as smoothing never masks a fault,” he said gently. “Visibility before beauty. Always.”

She met his eyes, then nodded.

“Always.”

Bren, fearless as ever but not careless, slid two fingers under one of Aldos’s copper bands and tested its give.

“Put me on safety detail during the Array,” he said. “Not just crowd flow—backstage lanes, rig ladders, catwalk checks. If something goes wrong, I’ll be where the first fall happens.”

“Nothing is going to fall,” Meli said.

“It won’t,” Bren agreed easily. “But I’ll be there anyway.”

Steam sighed. Aldos poured. They stood for a few breaths and let the warmth in their hands make the moment real.

The afternoon thinned toward evening. They took their cups to the bench by the west-facing panes, watching the schoolyard tilt golden. Plans came like tide—first gentle, then filling every space: rehearsal blocks, anchor tests, rune checks, a lunch to celebrate Bren’s letter at his parents’ house, a visit to Meli’s mother with the news. The lab door creaked. A runner from the bursar’s office appeared, winded, and handed Aldos a folded notice with a seal he didn’t recognize.

“From the city,” the runner said. “Grant committee.”

Aldos read it once, then again, slower. Invitation to present: Applied Alchemy for Public Clinics — Extraction Methods for Non-Mana Practitioners. Funds pending demonstration and safety review.

He didn’t speak. He just looked up at the two people who had somehow become the bracket around his days.

“Well?” Bren asked.

Aldos swallowed, then let a startled smile break its way out.

“They want to see the lab,” he said. “Our work.”

Bren whooped. Meli caught Aldos around the waist in a hug so sudden his cup almost tipped. He set it down and hugged her back with the awkwardness of a person learning how to do something he wanted to do all the time.

“Okay,” Meli said into his shoulder, then stepped back, eyes bright. “We’re doing this. All of it.”

They stayed until the panes went dark enough to mirror them. Meli practiced light—small things, fingertip flickers—while Aldos tuned a coil to a quieter hum and Bren marked a hazard lane with chalk on the floor and then immediately erased it because it interrupted the path to the kettle. When Meli finally slipped on her coat, Aldos walked them to the door.

“I’ll pull the old dome specs tonight,” he said. “I want to hear every place the glass sings.”

Meli glanced back, hand on the frame.

“You always say that—about machines singing.”

“They do,” he replied. “Even when we don’t want to hear it.”

Something in his tone made her search his face. It wasn’t worry—he didn’t show worry so bluntly. It was attention sharpened to a point.

“I’ll keep your sky steady,” he added, softer, like a promise he wasn’t ready to admit was a promise. “So it can breathe.”

She reached up, touched his cheek with two fingers, then let them fall.

“And I’ll make sure it remembers to be kind,” she said. “To them. To you.”

Her eyes tilted toward the high panes, where the last of twilight caught the glass. “It almost feels like the stars are aligning for us, doesn’t it?”

For a moment, Aldos just looked at her. The kind of look that made his chest feel too small for what it carried. A faint smile escaped him before he could stop it.

“You and those stars of yours,” he murmured.

The words softened something in her expression. A thread of laughter, a trace of relief. Like she’d been waiting to hear him say it. Bren pushed the door open with his shoulder, breaking the stillness with his usual ease.

“Tomorrow,” Meli said, stepping into the corridor. “Rehearsal block two. Don’t let me forget to eat.”

“I’ll bring the pastries,” Bren said.

“I’ll bring the protocols,” Aldos said.

“Of course you will,” she replied, smiling as she left.

When the lab settled again, the prism turned and turned, the same steady rhythm it had always kept. Aldos cleaned the chalk from the table, ran a cloth along the edge of a coil, then set a new page on his board. At the top he wrote, in neat hand:

Visibility before beauty.

Outside, the old dome threw back a faint thread of sound in the night air, a note so thin most would have missed it. He didn’t. He added a second line:

Glass—check for hairline fracture at north support.

Then he blew out the lamp and let the dark hold him a moment longer than usual—long enough to feel the shape of this new thing they were building: a sky they would raise together, and all the ways you keep a sky from breaking.

## Chapter 16

“I’m sorry,” he said.

He meant it.

He knew it wasn’t enough.

The silence that followed was heavier than shouting.

And as Bren’s footsteps faded down the gleaming hall, Aldos pressed his forehead against the cold column, the echo of Lucien’s smile still gnawing at him. For the first time, he had been outmaneuvered. For the first time, the world had told him he was powerless. And in that hollow, something darker began to take root.

## Chapter 21

Weeks slipped by in quiet labor. Time in Ranlean shifted almost without notice—lamps burning longer, lectures thinning, the city’s pulse steady as ever. A letter laid open on Aldos’s desk, its wax seal cracked clean through.

The Council of Ranlean hereby approves initial funding for an Unmorrowed Research Laboratory. We commend your persistence, ingenuity, and vision for a future where all citizens may contribute to the city’s progress.

The words should have tasted like victory. Instead, Aldos read them again and again, his lips tightening. Commendation without contrition. Praise without justice. They had given him a lab—but only after erasing Meli, after turning her name into a fund for strangers.

His fingers traced the glass jar on the corner of his desk, the faintest tremor still inside it, like something alive trying to get out. He told himself it was progress. He told himself it was what she would have wanted. But in the hollow of his chest, something was already unraveling.

Bren sat on the edge of his bed, travel pack at his feet. His armor had been polished, straps checked twice, boots laced tight. He wasn’t afraid of leaving Ranlean. He couldn’t be. But he felt the weight of it anyway—heavy, immovable, like a stone tied to memory.

His mother cupped his face, thumb brushing the line of his jaw.

“You’ll make us proud.”

His father’s embrace was quieter, steady as ever.

“Write when you can. And remember—you’ve got a home here.”

Bren nodded, but his eyes were already elsewhere.

On the far wall, above the dining alcove, a single handprint remained—Aldos’s. Pressed there during Hearthfall, smeared with spice and laughter, a careless mark in a house that had always been careful. Bren remembered his mother teasing, his father chuckling, Aldos trying to hide his smile. The day Aldos became family. That is what Bren was leaving. His chest tightened, though fear never came. Only a hollow echo where it should have lived.

He lingered in front of the wall, hand hovering near the print but not touching it. For a moment, he let himself imagine that Aldos might walk through the door again, tired coat on his shoulders, eyes heavy but alive. The silence told him otherwise. He shouldered his pack. Stepped outside. And for the first time, Bren admitted the truth:

That handprint was the last trace of Aldos Podrigo the world would ever know.

As for Aldos—he stared at the night sky, tracing the same stars Meli had left behind—yet in his eyes, the shimmer was gone.
